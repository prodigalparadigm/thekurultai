Where you put yourself

Staffing. You placed yourself at 30, near the pole that says people spend their paychecks. Read back: you want the work lighter and the roster intact. That means the archetype mix is grown in-house rather than bought, and MyWork carries real weight here, because four analysts and a nineteen-year ops lead are the whole capability and none of them have been asked what their job becomes.

Data governance. At 40 you are slightly left: cautious, not locked down. Read back: you do not want your data training anyone's model, but you are not standing up your own stack either. The posture that fits is a hosted model tier under contractual zero data retention and no-training terms, a single tenant with logging, and self-hosting reserved only for a specific customer contract that demands it. Check whether the two marketing licenses already carry those terms. If they do not, that is the first thing to fix, because unmanaged use is already happening.

Risk aversion. At 25 you are the careful one: public, and people read. One sentence of friction, said without scolding: two licenses running in marketing with no acceptable-use rules and a pilot that stopped without a write-up is not a careful posture, it is an unobserved one. Price both readings. The careful reading costs you a week of writing rules and turning on logs. The unobserved reading costs you whatever the first screenshot costs. Stage-gating below is the careful reading, operationalized.

Upfront investment. At 70 you want it cheap, and the intake agrees with you: you cannot hire engineers at your rates. So nothing here is a platform purchase. The spend is per-seat licenses with the right contract terms, plus a bounded partner engagement to get the close build finished and handed over. Build what only you can build, which is the close logic, and buy the model.

What the work frees up

You did not ask for headcount reduction, and the Kurultai would not produce it if you had. What this document produces instead is a capacity plan: every hour the work gives back is entered in the ledger as an explicit choice a named person makes by a named date. Your own words put it well, that people spend their paychecks. The specific risk worth pricing here is domain-knowledge erosion, and in your case it has a name: the ops lead who knows why every exception exists. If that knowledge is ever treated as a cost rather than an asset, you lose the ability to tell a good exception from a bad one, and no model will recover it, because the reasoning was never written down. That is why the dispatch work starts with recording reasons rather than replacing judgment. It is also worth saying plainly that the documented value in this field has come from adoption at zero layoffs, where capacity was redirected rather than removed. The two days a month are real. They are only a gain if somebody decides where they go before they arrive.

Non-negotiable: this is not a workforce-reduction plan. No slider setting changes that principle.

The four engines

Classification is from your own words, not from a benchmark. Where a process is mixed, the recommendation attaches only to the part that controls an outcome, and the rest is a conversation for management. Two of these four have a conversation attached precisely because tooling would make them worse.

Engine one
The ceremony audit

ProcessReadingWhat follows
Monthly close, which eats two daysmixed
Producing numbers that people act on is instrumental: it controls cash and commercial decisions downstream. Some of the two days is almost certainly instrumental work, and some is steps that run because they have always run, including reconciliations nobody has re-scoped since the system that required them changed. You do not yet know the split, because nobody has timed the steps.

Attached only to the instrumental part. Step one, before any tooling: map the close as a list of steps with hours against each, done by the people doing them. Step two: automate the pull-and-reconcile steps that are mechanical and leave judgment steps to a human reviewer, with every automated step producing a diff that a person signs off on for the first three cycles.

Controls: Elapsed days from period end to signed close, and the number of manual touches in the step map. Evidence: the step map at day 30 with hours, and the same map at day 90 showing which steps disappeared and which ones were kept on purpose.

For the management conversation: For the ceremonial part: ask which close outputs are read by someone who then does something different. Any step whose only consumer is the file itself is a candidate for retirement, and retiring it is a management decision, not an automation project.

Dispatch scheduling every morning on a whiteboardinstrumental
It is judged every day by whether loads move. That is an outcome test, so it is instrumental. The whiteboard is the complication: the record of what was decided and why evaporates each morning, which is why the exception knowledge lives in one person's head.

Capture before you model. For 30 days, record the morning board as structured data at the point of decision, including the reason when an assignment departs from the obvious one. Only after that, run draft assignments in suggest mode, visible to the ops lead, never auto-dispatched.

Controls: Coverage of loads and the rate of same-day reassignment, held flat or better. Evidence: a side-by-side log of drafted assignment versus the assignment actually made, with the ops lead's reason recorded when they differ. If the draft agrees with the ops lead often enough to be useful, you will see it in that log; if it does not, you stop.

Quarterly business reviews that take a week to assemblemixed
Assembling the pack is instrumental if the pack feeds a decision. The review event itself is frequently a status display running on precedent, and a week of analyst time is an expensive display. Only you know which it is here.

Attached only to the assembly part, and only after the close work lands. Generate the standing sections of the pack from the same outputs the close produces, so the pack is a view on data that already exists rather than a fresh extraction. Analysts write the commentary; the machine does not write the interpretation.

Controls: Analyst hours to assemble, and the count of decisions recorded in the review itself. Evidence: a timed log of assembly hours for the next two quarters, and the decision list from each review.

For the management conversation: Before spending anything on the assembly, ask what decision has been changed by a QBR in the last year. If the honest answer is none, the week is ceremony and the fix is to shorten the review, not to speed up the deck. Speeding up a ceremony gets you more ceremony, faster.

RFP responses, two people for a week each timeinstrumental
It is judged by whether you win work. That is as clean an outcome test as you have.

Build an answer library from past submitted responses, and assemble first drafts by retrieval against it. Pricing, commitments, service levels and anything contractual stay human-written and human-approved. The draft is a starting point, never a submission.

Controls: Hours per response and the number of responses you are able to submit per quarter, at a win rate that does not fall. Evidence: hours logged per response before and after, plus submitted count and win rate against the prior four quarters.

Engine three
The people arc

MyRunbook. MyRunbook: every person in scope writes down what they actually do all day, in sequence, with rough time against each step. Not the job description, the day. This is the only way to see what hands off to a machine and what cannot, and in your case it is also the only way to see what the ops lead knows. Nineteen years of knowing why every exception exists is an asset that currently exists in exactly one copy. Start the runbooks with the close and with dispatch.

MyWork. MyWork: having seen the day written down, each person works out what the job is now. This is not a performance exercise and it is not a prelude to anything. The answer is usually that the mechanical third goes and the judgment two thirds get more room. At a staffing placement of 30 this is the heaviest part of the program, because the roster is staying and the work is changing, which means the job descriptions have to change with it or people will simply absorb the slack and you will get no benefit.

The governance starter

Acceptable use, in skeleton

  • Approved tools only, under contracts with zero data retention and no training on your data. Personal accounts are not approved tools.
  • Named categories that never leave the approved tier: customer contract terms, pricing not yet quoted, personnel matters, anything covered by a customer confidentiality clause. Fill in the exact list with IT and the commercial lead.
  • Every output that reaches a customer, a counterparty or a financial record is reviewed and owned by a named person. The model is never the author of record.
  • Disclosure rule: say internally where a draft came from. A draft nobody admits is a draft nobody checks.
  • Logging is on by default and reviewed monthly by IT. Use that cannot be logged is not approved use.

Where a human stays in the loop, by default

  • Monthly close: every automated step produces a diff, and a human signs the diff, for the first three cycles minimum.
  • Dispatch: suggest only. The ops lead approves every assignment. No auto-dispatch inside the 90 days, regardless of how good the agreement rate looks.
  • RFP: human-authored for anything with a price, a commitment or a service level. The library drafts the descriptive sections only.
  • Anything that writes to a financial system of record requires two-person approval, the preparer and the reviewer.

Kill switch. One named person, in IT, can revoke access and stop all automated steps inside one business hour, without consulting anyone first. The switch is a documented procedure, not a login someone remembers. It is drilled once inside the 90 days and the drill is written up. The standing rule is that stopping is always allowed and never requires justification in advance; justification comes afterward, in the review.

Stage gate. Your placement at 25 means you run observe, then suggest, then act, and you do not skip. Observe means the system produces output nobody acts on while you measure accuracy. Suggest means a human sees the output and chooses. Act means the system proceeds unless stopped. Promotion from one stage to the next is a configuration change, not a culture change: it is approved by a named reviewer, which here is IT together with the process owner, it is recorded in the audit log with the date and the accuracy evidence that justified it, and it is reversible by the kill switch. Inside these 90 days, nothing reaches act except steps that are fully reversible and have no customer-visible effect. The close diffs and the dispatch drafts both stay at suggest.

The staged pattern that describes is a working one, not a diagram: observe, then suggest, then act, with promotion between stages a config change a named reviewer approves. stagegate is the implementation, with a hash-chained audit log and the kill switch wired in.

What this document does not know

  • Owner names in the ledger: Finance lead who owns the monthly close, and Commercial lead who owns RFP responses, are roles the work implies, not titles you gave us. Confirm who actually holds them before signing, and change the row if the holder is someone else.
  • Which steps of the close consume the two days? The step map at day 30 answers this, and until it exists every estimate of the achievable saving is a guess.
  • Do the two marketing licenses carry zero data retention and no-training terms? If not, what do they carry?
  • What stopped the pilot nobody finished? Budget, a technical wall, a person leaving, or a loss of sponsorship. The answer determines whether this program hits the same wall.
  • Has a QBR ever changed a decision? If not, the week of assembly is a ceremony and the fix is editorial, not technical.
  • What is your current RFP volume per quarter and your win rate? Without that baseline, the answer library has no outcome to be judged against.
  • Do any customer contracts restrict where their data can be processed or stored? This is the one thing that could force a different deployment posture than the hosted tier recommended here.
  • Who, specifically, holds the kill switch, and does that person have the authority to use it without asking first?
